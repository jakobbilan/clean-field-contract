The Clean Field Contract

A proposal to pay Punjab’s farmers for paddy fields left unburnt

Elchin Abbasov, Jakob Bilan, Timeo Goncalves, Narmin Isgandarli, Jack King, Ann Mary Philip and Cosimo Zatti

School of Public Policy, London School of Economics and Political Science
Policy brief, November 2026

Summary

  • Crop fires in Punjab cause 64% of India’s exposure to smoke from crop fires. Across India, that smoke was linked to between 44,000 and 98,000 early deaths a year in 2003–2019.1
  • The official count of farm fires in Punjab fell from 71,304 in 2021 to 5,114 in 2025. Satellite maps of burnt fields show a much smaller fall, and most large fires now burn after the satellites that count them have passed.234
  • Farmers burn because it is the quickest and cheapest way to clear the straw. In a randomised trial in Punjab, paying farmers not to burn reduced burning when part of the money was paid up front.15
  • We propose ₹2,500 an acre for a field left unburnt, ₹500 of it paid at enrolment, with every enrolled plot checked by satellite. Punjab would fund the 2027 season. From 2028 the Centre and Delhi would pay for each certified acre.

On 11 November 2021 a NASA satellite photographed a long plume of smoke flowing from fires in Punjab and Haryana towards Delhi.6 In October and November the prevailing winds over the plains blow from the north-west and carry the smoke over Haryana and Delhi.17

The shaded sector shows the directions in which a north-westerly wind blows.

The environment ministry estimated that farm fires contributed 48% of Delhi’s fine particulate pollution on 7 November 2021, the highest daily share that season.2

Crop fires in Punjab cause 64% of India’s exposure to crop-fire smoke, almost six times Haryana’s share of 11%.1 Nearly two-thirds of the exposure comes from burning after the rice harvest in October and November, when farmers have two to three weeks to clear the straw before wheat has to be sown.1

Under the national monitoring protocol, 71,304 farm fires were counted in Punjab between 15 September and 30 November 2021.2

Each dot is a fire detected in that season by the VIIRS instrument on the NOAA-20 satellite, detections in all.9 The season plays forward as you scroll.

The count fell every year after that, to 5,114 in 2025.10113 In 2026 it rose again, to 7,159 by 25 November.12

The dots now show the 2026 season, detections.

Six districts between the Sutlej and the Ghaggar, Barnala, Sangrur, Patiala, Moga, Ludhiana and Fatehgarh Sahib, caused about 40% of the health damage from crop burning in India over 2003–2019. Patiala and Sangrur alone caused 20%.113

These districts grow coarse rice varieties that leave more straw to burn, and they lie upwind of densely populated areas.1

A satellite records a fire as a point with coordinates and a time. It sees only fires that are burning at the moment it passes overhead, once or twice a day.4

On the ground, burning is the quickest and cheapest way to clear the straw before sowing wheat, and the smoke is carried to towns and cities downwind.1

What the fire counts miss

Punjab’s official fire count comes from instruments on polar-orbiting satellites that pass over the state in daylight at about 10:30 and 13:30 local time.4 Burnt area is measured differently, from optical images taken in the days after a fire, which show burn scars on the fields. Since 2021 the two measures have moved apart (Figure 1).

CEEW estimates that burnt area in Punjab fell by about 11% between 2022 and 2024, while the count fell by 78%.16 iFOREST’s maps show a fall of 37% between 2022 and 2025.4

iFOREST attributes much of the gap to timing. In 2021, 3% of large fires in Punjab burned after 3 pm. In 2024 and 2025 more than 90% did, according to observations taken every 15 minutes by a geostationary satellite.4 Researchers at the Indian Space Research Organisation, using another geostationary satellite, found that the daily peak of burning moved from about 1:30 pm in 2020 to about 5 pm in 2024 (Figure 2).17 A separate analysis at NASA found the same shift.1819

Later burning probably also does more harm. The lowest layer of the atmosphere, in which smoke mixes and thins out, is deepest at 1–2 pm and becomes shallower afterwards. Lan and colleagues estimate that burning two hours earlier in Punjab could avert up to 9,600 early deaths a year.1 Modelling also suggests that evening fires cause a stronger build-up of particles overnight.19

Enforcement that relies on the daytime count misses most large fires. The Commission for Air Quality Management now plans to rely on burnt-area estimates and has asked states to use drones to find fires that the satellites miss.20 The contract proposed here also pays on mapped burnt area, checked against a second source.

Why farmers burn

After harvesting rice in October or November, a Punjab farmer has two to three weeks to clear the field and sow wheat.1 Sowing a week late costs about 1.5 quintals of wheat an acre, against an average yield of 22.21 The alternatives to fire need a machine at the busiest time of year, either a seeder that sows wheat through the straw or a baler that removes it (Figure 3).

Burning costs the farmer nothing in money, while the harm from the smoke falls on other people. Fines have been small next to the cost of the alternatives: in 2018–19, fines collected in Punjab came to ₹29 for each landholding estimated to have burned.23 By 25 November this year the state had registered 2,188 police cases over stubble burning, with 7,159 fires counted.12

Punjab has tried bans, fines, machine subsidies and cash payments.

Fines rely on detecting fires, and most large fires now burn after the satellites have passed. Machine subsidies make the alternatives cheaper but pay nothing for using them. Only the 2019 scheme paid farmers for not burning, and it relied on their own declarations.

What the evidence shows

In 2019 a randomised trial with 1,668 farmers in 171 villages of Bathinda and Faridkot tested payments for not burning. The farmers had used equipment the year before that suggested they burned their straw. Most contracts paid ₹800 an acre, and one variant paid ₹1,600. In some villages the whole payment came after verification. In others a quarter or half was paid up front and kept whatever the farmer did.5

Paying part up front raised the share of farmers who burned none of their plots by 7.7 to 11.5 percentage points, depending on how the satellite images were classified. Paying only after verification had no measurable effect. The authors estimate a cost of $3,600 to $5,400 per life saved.5

The authors suggest two reasons why the advance helps: it shows farmers that the final payment will come, and it gives them cash to hire machines. It worked no better for the farmers with the least cash or the least trust, so which reason matters more is not settled. Most farmers still burned, which the authors read as a sign that ₹800 an acre was below most farmers’ full cost of the alternatives.5

The proposal

The Clean Field Contract pays farmers for an outcome, a paddy field with no burn scar, and leaves the method to them.

Who can joinEvery grower of non-basmati paddy in a covered village, owner or tenant. Joining does not change any fine a farmer may face.
At enrolment₹500 an acre, paid into the farmer’s bank account within three working days of approval. The farmer keeps it even if the field burns, but then receives no advance the following season.
After the season₹2,000 an acre if none of the farmer’s registered paddy plots shows a burn scar. Seeders, balers, fodder and sale to a straw buyer all count.
Acres coveredThe full rate on the first 10 acres and half the rate on acres 10 to 25, so no farmer receives more than ₹43,750 a season.
ChecksSentinel-2 burnt-area maps cover every enrolled plot. A plot flagged as burnt is checked against a second, independent source, and payment is refused only if both show a burn, because satellite data alone are not a sound basis for such decisions.29 Field staff inspect a random sample of plots before tilling, after which burnt and unburnt plots look alike.5 Farmers can appeal within 15 days.
PaymentThrough Anaaj Kharid, the online portal Punjab uses for its paddy and wheat procurement.30
TenantsA tenant registers the plots he farms. The owner is told by text message, and plot lists are posted in the village. A claim that nobody contests within seven days stands. It records who farms the plot for this payment only and creates no tenancy right.

₹2,500 an acre is close to the median price of renting a Super Seeder in 2022, ₹2,200,22 and equals the rate Punjab and Delhi proposed in 2022.25

Who pays

Punjab pays for the first season, so the scheme can start in 2027 without waiting for other governments. From 2028, if an independent evaluation finds that enrolled villages burn at least 5 percentage points less than villages still waiting to join, the Centre and Delhi take over the final payment, at ₹1,500 and ₹500 for each certified clean acre. Punjab continues to pay the advance and to run the scheme.

The Centre and Delhi pay only for acres that pass the checks. The same rate could be offered to any state that adopts the same checks.

Benefits and costs

The main benefit is fewer early deaths from smoke. In our model each acre that stops burning averts about 0.008 deaths a year, based on Lan and colleagues’ estimate of 380 deaths for each 1% cut in Punjab’s post-monsoon burning.131

Our central assumption is a 10-point effect on all enrolled land. The trial measured 7.7 to 11.5 points at ₹800 an acre, but only among farmers likely to burn; the contract pays more than three times as much, to every paddy grower who enrols.5 At 10 points about 5.4 lakh fewer acres burn and about 4,300 early deaths are averted each year once the scheme covers all of Punjab.31

Payments to farmers are transfers between groups. The costs that count are administration, farmers’ own costs of switching and the cost of raising public money. The net present value over 2027–2036 at a 9.5% discount rate is positive in every case we ran: about ₹18,000 crore in a cautious case, ₹35,600 crore with an Indian value of a statistical life, and ₹1.27 lakh crore with the workbook’s central values.31

Most of the money goes to farmers whose behaviour does not change, because they would not have burned or because they burn and keep the advance. In our model, ₹621 crore of the ₹696 crore paid to farmers each year would be paid even if no farmer changed what he does. Public money per early death averted is still about ₹17 lakh.31

Delivery

The scheme runs on systems Punjab already has: Anaaj Kharid for registration and payment, and satellite burnt-area maps for checks, with lists certified by the Commission for Air Quality Management. Half of Punjab’s villages, chosen by lottery, start in 2027 and the rest join in 2028, which allows a fair comparison (Figure 8).30

What has to happen

Punjab notifies the contract in December 2026, before the election code applies. Farmers in half of Punjab’s villages enrol from June 2027. Satellites and field staff check every enrolled plot after the harvest. If burning falls by at least 5 percentage points, the Centre and Delhi pay for each certified acre from 2028.
